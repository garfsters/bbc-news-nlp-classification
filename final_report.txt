Project 3: NLP for News Category Classification (Multi-Class)
Pace University -- Introduction to Deep Learning, Spring 2026

FINAL REPORT

OVERVIEW
--------
For this project I built a text classification system that categorizes BBC news
articles into five categories: business, entertainment, politics, sport, and
tech. The dataset had 2,225 articles total, pretty evenly spread across all
five categories. Sport and business had the most articles (511 and 510) and
entertainment had the fewest (386), but overall the distribution was balanced
enough that it did not cause any major issues for the models.

The pipeline I built involved cleaning the raw text using NLTK, converting it
into numerical features using TF-IDF, and then training two models: Logistic
Regression and a Linear SVM. Both ended up working really well.

MODEL PERFORMANCE
-----------------
Both models did better than I expected honestly:

  - Logistic Regression Accuracy: 98.20%
  - SVM Accuracy:                 98.43%

SVM came out slightly ahead, but the difference was pretty small. I think both
models did so well because the BBC dataset is clean, balanced, and each
category tends to use pretty distinct vocabulary. Sports articles sound nothing
like tech articles, so TF-IDF had an easy time picking up on those differences.

CONFUSION MATRIX ANALYSIS
--------------------------
Looking at where the models made mistakes was actually pretty interesting:

- Sport had zero misclassifications on both models, 102 out of 102 correct.
  That makes sense because sports articles are full of specific terms like
  player names, team names, and sport-specific language that does not really
  show up in other categories.

- Tech got mixed up with business a couple of times on both models. That one
  is pretty understandable since a lot of tech articles are about companies,
  earnings, and products, which overlaps with business coverage.

- Politics got confused with entertainment twice. This is probably from
  articles about political figures who also have a public persona, or stories
  about entertainment industry regulation.

- Entertainment had the fewest articles and one misclassification per model,
  which made it the trickiest category, though still classified very accurately.

STRENGTHS
---------
TF-IDF turned out to be a solid choice for this kind of task. It does a good
job of weighing words that are important to a specific article while
downweighting common words that show up everywhere. Both Logistic Regression
and SVM are also known to work well with the kind of sparse, high-dimensional
data that TF-IDF produces, so that combination made sense here.

The dataset itself also helped a lot. It is clean, well-labeled, and balanced,
which gave the models a fair shot at learning each category without being
biased toward the more common ones.

LIMITATIONS
-----------
The biggest limitation is that TF-IDF does not understand meaning, it just
counts words. So if the same word shows up in two different contexts it gets
treated the same way regardless. It also does not pick up on word order, so
the structure of a sentence does not factor in at all.

Another issue is that some articles probably span more than one topic, like a
story about a tech company getting investigated by the government. Those kinds
of articles are hard to classify into just one category, and the model has no
way to handle that.

POTENTIAL IMPROVEMENTS
----------------------
The most straightforward improvement would be switching from TF-IDF to word
embeddings like Word2Vec or GloVe, which actually capture the meaning of words
rather than just their frequency. Going even further, a model like BERT would
probably push accuracy very close to 100% on this dataset since it understands
context much better.

On the evaluation side, using cross-validation instead of a single train-test
split would give a more reliable picture of how the models actually perform.
Tuning hyperparameters like the C value in SVM would also be worth trying if
squeezing out a bit more performance was the goal.

CONCLUSION
----------
Overall I am happy with how this project turned out. Both models hit nearly
perfect accuracy, with SVM edging out Logistic Regression by a small margin.
It was a good reminder that you do not always need a complex model to get
strong results. With clean data, solid preprocessing, and the right feature
extraction method, even simpler models like Logistic Regression can perform
really well on text classification tasks.
